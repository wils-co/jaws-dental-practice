Dental implants

Dental implants

An implant is a way to replace a missing tooth. The fixture sits in the bone, a connector sits on top, and a crown (or bridge, or denture) is what you chew with. This page is about that treatment at Smiles by Design in Wheelers Hill, with Dr Julia Nguyen.

Some implant work is assessed and restored here. Who places the fixture (Julia, or a surgeon she refers to) is something she will confirm with you after she has looked. A website should not guess that.

What it is

A dental implant is a small post, usually titanium or a similar material, placed in the jaw where a tooth root used to be. Bone can grow against it over time. Once that is stable enough, a crown is attached.

It is not a same-day product and it is not a spare natural tooth. Healing takes months. Some people need extra procedures first, such as a graft, because there isn’t enough bone for a fixture to sit safely.

One missing tooth is the usual starting point on this page. Several missing teeth, or a mouth that needs a larger rebuild, is a different plan. That may mean more than one implant, a bridge on implants, or All-on-4. Julia will say which conversation you are actually in.

Who it may suit

This may be worth discussing if:

  • You have a missing tooth, or one that cannot be kept
  • The gums around the space are in reasonable health, or gum treatment can be done first
  • You can commit to the visits, the healing time, and the cleaning an implant needs for the rest of its life
  • You want a fixed option rather than a removable denture, or a bridge that relies on neighbouring teeth

It may not be the right time, or the right treatment, if bone is very limited, if gum disease is active, if medical history makes surgery a poor idea, or if you would rather a simpler option. Smoking, some medicines, and some health conditions change the risk. That is a consult conversation, not a yes from a webpage.

Julia will assess this in person. This page is not a diagnosis.

What happens

  1. Consult. What you want, what’s already in the mouth, medical history, and whether an implant is even on the table. Options and risks sit in this visit, not after you’ve said yes.
  2. Records. Photos, models or a scan, and X-rays. A 3D scan is used when it changes the plan (nearby nerves, sinus, bone volume). We’ll say why before it’s taken.
  3. Written plan. What would be placed, where, how many visits, what could go wrong, and a guide cost. You can take that home. Nothing is booked as surgery until you’ve had that in writing.
  4. Placement. The fixture is placed in the bone under local anaesthetic, unless a different setting has been planned. Stitches, a temporary tooth if one is being used, and written aftercare.
  5. Healing. Often two to four months, sometimes longer. You may wear a temporary in that time. The implant can fail in this window. That is uncommon, and it is a real risk.
  6. The tooth. An abutment and crown (or other restoration) are made and fitted. Bite and cleaning space get checked. Then recalls, not a farewell.

Where this is done: Assessment, planning, and the crown or denture that goes on top are part of care at Smiles by Design. Surgical placement is confirmed at consult: in this practice, or with a referred colleague, with Julia still involved in the plan. We will not describe a full in-house surgical pathway until that is actually how Julia works.

There is no in-house general anaesthetic here. If you need treatment asleep, that is a referral.

Risks and recovery

Implants are surgery. They are not guaranteed, and they are not maintenance-free.

Risks can include:

  • Swelling, bruising, bleeding, and a sore jaw or sinus after placement
  • Infection around the site
  • The implant not taking in the bone (early failure), or failing later (including peri-implantitis, which is gum and bone disease around an implant)
  • Injury to a nerve, which can mean altered feeling in the lip, chin, or tongue; this is uncommon and it matters, especially in the lower jaw
  • Sinus problems when an implant is planned in the upper back teeth
  • Need for extra bone or gum grafting, which has its own risks and healing
  • The crown, screw, or connector loosening, chipping, or wearing, and needing repair or remake
  • The implant being the wrong choice if gum disease, bite, or cleaning isn’t managed

Recovery is usually a few days of softness and swelling, then weeks of quiet healing you cannot see. Time off work is often short; it still depends on the case. Call if pain, swelling, or bleeding is getting worse rather than better, or if you have a fever, numbness that isn’t settling, or a loose fixture or crown.

Treatment risks

Other options

  • Leave the space, if the bite and the neighbouring teeth allow it
  • A bridge that uses the teeth on either side
  • A partial denture
  • A different implant plan (more fixtures, a delayed placement, or a full-arch option such as All-on-4)
  • A second opinion

Doing nothing is a choice. So is waiting. Julia will not talk you into an implant because it is on the homepage.

Aftercare

For the first days: soft food, no smoking, careful cleaning around the site as instructed, and the medicines you’ve been given if any. Don’t poke the stitches. Don’t treat the implant like a natural tooth the week it’s placed.

Long term, an implant still needs brushing, cleaning between the teeth, and checks. Food traps and gum inflammation around implants are common if cleaning is missed. Night-time grinding can break components; a splint is sometimes part of the plan.

FAQs

Does it hurt?

Placement is done with local anaesthetic, so you shouldn’t feel the surgery itself. Afterwards it is usually sore, bruised, and a bit tight, more like an extraction than a filling. We do not call this pain-free. If you are anxious, say so; we can go slower. Sleep dentistry and general anaesthetic are not offered in-house.

How long does it take?

From first visit to a finished crown is often several months, not several days. Extra bone work, gum treatment, or a tooth that must come out first adds time. Anyone quoting a single “typical” number as the usual course is simplifying it. You’ll get a sequence that matches your mouth.

What does it cost?

Implant fees vary with how many fixtures, whether a graft is needed, whose surgery it is, and what sits on top. Guide figures and what changes the number are on dental implant cost. A plan for you is written after an exam. Health funds and payment plans may help with some item numbers; they are not a quote, and they don’t decide the clinical choice.

How much are dental implants?

There isn’t a single figure. One tooth is a different plan to several, or to All-on-4. How we build the number, including Australia-wide “how much” questions, sits on dental implant cost. No number on this page is a quote.

How long do dental implants last?

The fixture can last many years if the bone, gums, and cleaning stay healthy. The crown, screw, and connector wear and can need repair or remake sooner. Nothing here is a lifetime guarantee. Grinding, smoking, and gum disease shorten the odds. Your written plan should say what we would do if a part fails.

Can I use my super for dental implants?

Early release of superannuation is a government process with its own eligibility rules. The clinic does not approve or refuse your application. Medicare does not generally pay for dental implants in a private practice. Details, and what we will and won’t do on paper, are on dental implant cost.
